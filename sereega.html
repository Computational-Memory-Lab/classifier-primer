Simulating EEG you already know the answer to

Every number elsewhere on this site is an estimate of something we cannot observe. Simulation inverts that: you decide what the brain is doing, generate the recording it would produce, and then check whether your analysis can find what you put there. If it cannot, the problem is the analysis.

This page is a working model, not the toolbox. Everything here runs live in your browser and reimplements the shape of what SEREEGA does. The head and the electrode positions are real — taken from EEGLAB's own standard head model — but the conduction physics is simplified, and it is not SEREEGA. The section what this demo is not is specific about the difference, and it matters.

The one equation everything hangs off

Simulating EEG means answering: if some patch of cortex does something, what appears at an electrode taped to the scalp? That is the forward problem, and it has a compact form:

x  =  As  +  ε what the electrodes record = (how sources reach the scalp) × (what the sources do) + noise

Three pieces, and you supply all three:

piecewhat it iswho decides it
s — source activity What each simulated generator does over time: an ERP peak, an oscillation, background noise. You. This is the ground truth.
A — the lead field A table saying how strongly each source reaches each electrode. Physics: skull thickness, tissue conductivity, geometry. A head model. SEREEGA ships several.
ε — noise Everything not modelled as a source. You, by choosing its colour and size.

The reason this is worth doing: you know s. When a classifier reaches 0.53 on real data you cannot tell whether the effect is weak, the pipeline is lossy, or there are too few trials. Here you set the effect yourself, so every gap between what you planted and what came out is attributable.

The head model: one source, and what the scalp sees

Start with a single generator — a current dipole, the standard model of a small patch of cortex firing together. It has a position and a direction, and both matter enormously.

Drag the head to rotate it. Move the dipole with the sliders and watch the scalp pattern. Three things are worth deliberately trying:

  • Point it straight out (elevation 90°) versus sideways (0°). A dipole pointing at the scalp gives one compact blob. A dipole lying tangential gives a pair of opposite-signed regions with nothing in the middle — so the strongest signal appears where the source is not.
  • Push it deep. The pattern gets weaker and much broader. Deep sources are hard to see and harder to localise.
  • Drop from 128 electrodes to 19. Watch the sampling get coarse enough to miss structure that is plainly there in the surface.

Blue is negative, orange positive. Rings are electrodes, filled with the value they would record. Drag the head to rotate.
The geometry is real; the physics is still an approximation. The surface is the scalp boundary from EEGLAB's standard head model — the Colin27 template, by way of FieldTrip — and the electrodes are actual 10-05 coordinates from the same model, so Cz really is at the vertex and the montage really is a montage. What is not real is the conduction: this computes a dipole in a uniform medium, with no poorly-conducting skull between source and sensor. A real lead field would spread and attenuate everything further. Shapes and relationships are right; absolute numbers are not.

This is why a scalp map is not a picture of the brain. A single source can produce two opposite-signed patches, neither centred on it. Two sources produce a sum that may resemble neither. Going from scalp back to sources is the inverse problem, and it has no unique solution — which is exactly why simulation is useful, since it is the one setting where you know the answer.

The part that makes it realistic: nothing repeats exactly

If every trial were identical, averaging would be pointless and classification trivial. Real trials vary, and SEREEGA makes variability a first-class parameter. Every value can carry:

  • a deviation — random spread from trial to trial;
  • a slope — systematic drift across the session, i.e. fatigue;
  • a probability of appearance — the component sometimes simply does not happen.

The figure below has the first two of those. Set the noise high and the trials low, and the average is unreliable. Raise the trial count and it firms up — as the square root, which is the whole reason ERP research averages.

Single trials, and the average that emerges · live
Dashed blue is the truth you planted. Orange is the average of the noisy trials. Grey is the individual trials a classifier actually sees.
Watch the third statistic. Averaging improves signal-to-noise by the square root of the trial count — 100 trials buys a factor of 10, and 400 buys 20. That diminishing return is the same square-root wall that the trial-count page runs into from the classification side.

Now decode it

Put the two halves together. Two conditions differ in one thing only — the amplitude of the late component — and everything else is shared noise. The source is projected through the head model to every electrode, each channel is reduced to windowed means exactly as our real pipeline does, and a classifier is trained and tested on the result.

The classifier here is diagonal-covariance LDA, which is what Γ = 1 reduces to — the setting our own study-phase pipeline runs. It is fitted on half the trials and scored on the other half. Nothing is precomputed; this really is classifying the simulated data.

Two things to try, because they are the whole argument of this project:

  1. Raise the electrode count with everything else fixed. More electrodes means more signal — and also more features to estimate. Watch which wins.
  2. Set the effect small and the trials low. The AUC collapses toward 0.5 even though the effect is definitely there, because you put it there. That is the entire study-phase problem in one control.

Orange points are the fixed ladder of trial counts; the blue marker is wherever your trials slider sits. Every point is fitted on exactly that many trials — no reuse.

Set the effect to zero and the curve should sit around 0.5, scattering more at low trial counts because the held-out half is small. Measured over 20 seeds this build averages 0.4946 (t = −0.63 against chance), and the check is pinned by a test in site/test/. If a simulation cannot produce chance from no effect, nothing else it says is worth anything — an earlier version of this page sat at 0.519 because the class label was tied to the random-number stream, which is exactly the sort of thing the null check exists to catch.

The trap the authors warn about themselves

SEREEGA's own paper contains a caveat that anyone using it for classification has to design around, and it is worth quoting exactly:

"when SEREEGA is used to model a linear effect, and a linear method is subsequently used to again identify this effect, the obtained results could be essentially tautological."

Krol et al. (2018), Discussion.

Which is precisely our situation. We plant an amplitude difference — a linear effect — and decode it with LDA and linear SVM, which are linear methods. That the classifier finds it is not evidence about the brain. It is evidence that arithmetic works.

So what is a simulation like this legitimately for? Not for showing an effect is decodable — that was assumed. It is for measuring how much of a known effect survives a given pipeline: how AUC falls with fewer trials, how much a montage costs, how much a preprocessing choice throws away. The ground truth is a ruler, not a result.

It also sets a standard our project holds itself to: any claim that the pipeline is adequate has to be tested against an effect that is not a linear amplitude difference, since that is the one case a linear classifier is guaranteed to handle.

What our own runs found

Everything above is a toy. The real runs used SEREEGA proper on the cluster — a parietal dipole carrying a late-positive component, 64 brown-noise background sources, a lead field built for our actual 257-channel geodesic net, and SEREEGA's own multi-participant recipe (each participant's source moved within 20 mm and its orientation perturbed ±25%). Four results.

The montage is costing us, and our ten are still well chosen

Two findings in one table. The repo's ten beat every random ten by 0.08–0.14, so that choice is genuinely good rather than arbitrary. But 64 electrodes buys +0.07 over them and the full set buys +0.14 — so ten is leaving real AUC on the table, and more electrodes wins even though the feature count grows from 120 to 4,068. Signal beats estimation burden here, which is not what the trial-count argument alone would predict.

One caveat, and it is a large one. Linear SVM does not follow LDA up that axis — it flatlines near 0.56 while LDA climbs to 0.96. Any conclusion about montage from this table is a conclusion about LDA specifically.

A physically generated effect lands in the high-variance directions

In the abstract simulations we chose where the effect sat among the features. Here physics chose: a parietal dipole projects to whatever scalp topography it projects to. The result was that 51–70% of the effect's energy landed in the top 10% of covariance directions, against the 0.10 a random orientation gives — a five- to sevenfold enrichment.

That matters because the abstract work had shown a top-aligned effect recovers 91% of its ceiling at 1,800 trials while a randomly oriented one recovers 55%. A real effect is not perfectly aligned — it still needs about 35 directions to carry 90% of its energy — but it is much closer to the good end than the random end.

The simulator does not reproduce real non-Gaussianity

Real study-phase features are wildly heavy-tailed — kurtosis 23 against the Gaussian 3, with individual participants past 100. The forward model produces features that are almost exactly Gaussian, and the reason is unavoidable: sum 64 brown-noise sources through a lead field and the central limit theorem gives you a Gaussian.

Second-order structure reproduces well; higher-order structure does not. Effective dimension matches closely (12.0 against 9.5), so the covariance the classifier exploits is realistic. What is missing is the tails — the artifacts, bursts and blinks that survive preprocessing. LDA and linear SVM use only the first two moments, so the direct effect should be modest, but heavy tails inflate covariance estimates through outliers. This is a standing caveat on every simulated AUC we report.

Two traps, recorded so nobody rediscovers them

  • EEGLAB ships a partial copy of FieldTrip that shadows the real one and breaks lead-field reading. The paths have to be stripped after loading EEGLAB and before initialising FieldTrip properly.
  • A FieldTrip lead field carries no default dipole orientations. Our first run produced pure noise, silently, because every source had a zero moment. It ran to completion and returned plausible-looking chance results. If a simulation returns exactly chance, suspect the generator before the classifier.

What this demo is not

Stated plainly, because an interactive page is persuasive in a way that has nothing to do with being right.

this pageSEREEGA proper
head geometry real — the Colin27 scalp surface from EEGLAB's standard head model the same family of template heads
conduction a dipole in a uniform medium: no skull, no tissue boundaries boundary-element or finite-element lead fields with separate brain, skull and scalp conductivities
source space one dipole you place by hand ~75,000 candidate locations, oriented perpendicular to the cortical surface
channels real 10-05 coordinates, 19 to 128 of them real montages up to 228 channels, or any montage via FieldTrip
noise 1/fn summed sinusoids at each channel independently placed noise sources projected through the same lead field
missing entirely skull attenuation, eye and muscle artifacts, connectivity between sources connectivity yes; eye and muscle now available via HArtMuT

The qualitative behaviour is faithful: dipolar topographies, the depth-versus-spread relationship, radial against tangential orientation, averaging as the square root of trials, and classification collapsing when trials run short. Absolute amplitudes are not — the missing skull is the main reason, and it is a large one, since the skull is roughly eighty times less conductive than the tissue either side of it. Do not read a µV figure off this page and use it for anything.

One more limitation shared with the real thing, from the section above: neither this page nor our cluster runs reproduce the heavy tails of real EEG. Both are more Gaussian than the data they stand in for.
