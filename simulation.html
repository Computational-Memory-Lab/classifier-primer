How much data would it take?

Our classifiers reach about 0.53. The obvious explanation is that we do not have enough data — so we simulated brains where we knew the right answer, and asked exactly how much would be enough.

This page grows with the project. Everything below is current as of the runs described, and figures marked live compute in your browser from the same formulas the cluster runs used. Where a result was withdrawn, it says so rather than disappearing.

The thing that limits us is not what we assumed

A classifier looking at study-phase EEG gets 120 numbers per trial — ten electrodes at twelve time points. It has to work out how much weight to put on each of those 120 numbers. To do that it uses the trials you give it.

Each participant studies 225 words. Cross-validation holds some back, so the classifier learns from about 202. That is 202 examples to pin down 120 weights — fewer than two examples per weight. Its answer is mostly noise.

How badly this bites depends on how strong the memory effect is, and it depends on it squared. Halve the effect and you need four times the trials. Our effect is small, so the requirement grows fast.

Simulation gave a closed form, and it matched the measured curves to about 0.007 AUC across the whole grid:

trials needed  =  4 × (number of features) ÷ (effect size)2

Set the ceiling to 0.531 (our study-phase number), features to 120 and trials to 225 — the actual experiment. Then try raising trials alone, and watch how little it buys. Then drop features instead.

Trials are not the lever. At our effect size, going from 225 to 1,800 trials — an eight-fold longer experiment — moves AUC by about 0.06. Halving the feature count is worth roughly a quarter of that, for free.

There is a second lever, and it turned out to matter more than the first. It is not how many numbers the classifier gets but where the memory signal sits among them.

Understanding why needs a working picture of what these two classifiers actually compute — what a weight is, and where its value comes from. That is its own subject, and it has its own pages: LDA, from the ground up and SVM, from the ground up, written to be read side by side with no machine-learning background assumed.

What the runs found

Two stages so far. Stage 1 used made-up numbers where the right answer was known exactly. Stage 2 simulated an actual electrical source in a model head, so that physics — not us — decided where the memory signal landed.

Confirmed

  • The trial requirement scales as 4p/Δ². Simulation matched the closed form to a median of 0.007 AUC across the grid.
  • Fewer features buys real AUC. At 1,800 trials: 30 features reach 0.634, 240 features reach 0.579.
  • Where the signal sits decides everything. Same effect size, three placements, 1,800 trials: in the components that vary most it recovers 91% of the ceiling; spread evenly, 55%; in the components that barely vary, 7% — indistinguishable from chance.
  • A physically generated memory effect lands in the good place. About 70% of its energy falls in the top tenth of components, where an arbitrary signal would put 10% there.
  • The ten electrodes we use are well chosen. They beat random tens by 0.08–0.14. But 64 electrodes reach 0.90 against their 0.82, and twenty electrodes spread to the edges do no better than the ten — placement, not count.

Withdrawn

Two predictions made during this work turned out to be wrong, and the runs are what showed it.

  • That our scoring method was deflating the numbers. Measured at −0.001. It is unbiased. It adds noise, not bias.
  • That correlated features would cut the requirement 7–12×. Real features carry only ~9.5 independent dimensions out of 120, which looked like a large saving. It is not: a signal spread evenly across all directions gains nothing, because the ones that barely vary are exactly the ones estimated worst. The saving is real only for a signal that concentrates where the variance is — which, per Stage 2, a real one does.

Known limitation

The simulated features are almost perfectly smooth; real ones are not. A standard measure of how often extreme values occur reads 3 for well-behaved data. Our simulation gives 2.9. Real recordings give 23, and some participants exceed 100 — blinks, muscle activity and transients that survive cleaning. Second-order structure reproduces well; the tails do not. Every simulated number here carries that caveat.

The conclusion

Putting the formula together with where real effects sit gives a defensible number rather than a worst case. Read the placement result as an effective feature count — the number that reproduces what we measured. A signal concentrated in the components that vary most behaves like 31 features rather than 120; one spread evenly behaves like 318, which is worse than the nominal count.

Why every pipeline comparison we have run came out flat

At 225 trials with 120 features and a 0.531 effect, the classifier recovers 7.5% of the effect it is given. Attenuation that severe does not merely lower the number — it compresses the differences between pipelines by the same factor.

So if one preprocessing choice genuinely beat another, we would measure:

With 62 participants we can resolve about 0.02–0.03. Put the two together: a preprocessing change would have to be worth roughly 0.07 AUC in truth before it became visible to us at all — and no preprocessing change is worth that.

So the near-identical rows in our parameter sweeps — raw, filtered and Laplacian within 0.001–0.014 of each other — were never evidence that those choices do not matter. They are what this design produces whether or not the choices matter. The sweeps could not have resolved a realistic difference, and running more of them at this trial count cannot change that.

That is the main result, and it is a statement about the experiment's design rather than about preprocessing.

What we tested on real data, and what it showed

If the signal concentrates in about ten components, compressing the 120 features down to those ten before classifying should keep the signal and cut the estimation burden. We ran it on two datasets.

  • Study phase (1.7 training examples per weight): compression gave +0.012, peaking at 10–20 components — exactly the predicted shape. But p = 0.21, below the 0.025 this dataset can resolve. It could not answer.
  • Feedback task (19 examples per weight): compression gave +0.0002, p = 0.96. Nothing — on a dataset with the power to detect something.

Which is the predicted result, not a failed prediction. Compression is a remedy for having more weights than examples, and the feedback task does not have that problem. The intervention helps where the theory says it should and is inert where it says it should be. It is a targeted fix, not a general improvement to bolt onto the pipeline.

What follows

  1. Stop running pipeline comparisons at 225 trials. They cannot answer the question asked of them, and a null from them means nothing.
  2. The levers are effect size and effective dimensionality, in that order. Trials enter linearly; the effect enters squared. A paradigm change that doubles the effect beats any achievable increase in session length.
  3. A learned low-dimensional representation is the one intervention that moves the right quantity. It attacks effective dimensionality directly, which no amount of extra recording does.

Every figure here assumes near-Gaussian features, and real ones are not (kurtosis 23 against the simulator's 2.9). Treat these as accurate in order of magnitude and in their ratios, not in the third decimal.

What was already known

The formula above is not new. Almost all of it was worked out between the late 1960s and 1991, and we found it again by simulating our way into it. That changes what this project's contribution is — and because agreeing with established theory is much better evidence that the simulator works than a novel result would be.

Raudys & Jain (1991)

The canonical treatment. Their equation 16 gives the amount by which a classifier's expected error exceeds its best-possible error, and it carries the same structure we derived: it falls as 1/N, and the terms in the numerator carry the feature count. They state outright that the number of trials needed scales linearly with features for a linear classifier and quadratically for a quadratic one. So our trials = 4 × features ÷ effect² is a rediscovery of the Raudys–Deev asymptotics.

Their Table I is the practical part. It gives the trials needed to keep expected error within 50% of its best-possible value:

Trials required, by how much the classifier estimates
classifierwhat it estimatestrials needed
Euclideanclass means only1.2 × features
Fisher LDAmeans + one shared covariance4.0 × features
Quadraticmeans + a covariance per class10–32 × features
Raudys & Jain (1991), Table I, at a best-possible error of 10%. The quadratic row grows faster than linearly, which is why it is a range.

Three things follow, and all three matter for us.

We are below even the cheapest row. Fisher LDA at 120 features wants about 480 trials. We train on 202. The pipeline is not merely under-sampled for the classifier it runs — it is under-sampled for the simplest classifier available.

The gap between the first two rows is independent confirmation of the shrinkage result. Not estimating a covariance at all costs 1.2 × features instead of 4.0 — a factor of 3.3. That is the same effect our Γ sweep and the compression test found by simulation, derived analytically thirty-five years earlier. It also reframes the Γ = 1 in our published analysis: the pipeline is being pushed toward the top row of that table by its own shortage of trials, and at 1.7 trials per weight that is the defensible place to be, not an arbitrary one.

Their numbers and ours agree once effect size is matched. "480 trials" looks reassuring next to our 10,100, and it would be fair to read that as us being wrong. We are not. Their table is computed for a strong effect — a 10% error rate, far stronger than anything in memory EEG. Evaluate our own formula at that same effect and we get 311 trials against their 480: the same size, theirs a little stricter because its bar is tighter. Our large number comes from our effect being 0.531 rather than from different theory, and the requirement grows as the square of how weak the effect is.

What is actually ours

Not the formula. What this project adds is the part the theory cannot supply on its own — the measurements that turn it into a statement about this experiment:

  • the effective dimensionality of real memory-EEG features, measured rather than assumed;
  • the finding that where the effect sits among the features matters more than how many there are;
  • the consequence for our own published sweeps — that this design could not have resolved the pipeline differences it was built to compare.

Two papers we have not read yet

Recording this because it is the honest state, not the tidy one. Blankertz et al. (2011) is the closest published treatment of our exact setting: shrinkage LDA on single-trial ERP features, with too few trials as its central concern. It may already answer what our Γ sweep was built to ask, and until someone reads it that part of this page should be treated as unsettled. Combrisson & Jerbi (2015) is on how easily decoding accuracy exceeds chance by chance — directly relevant to reading anything above 0.5 as real.
