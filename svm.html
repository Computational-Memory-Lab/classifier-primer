SVM, from the ground up

A support vector machine produces the same kind of answer as LDA — a weighted sum of the features — and arrives at it by reasoning that has nothing in common with LDA's. Where LDA models how the data were produced, SVM ignores that question entirely and asks a geometric one instead.

No machine learning assumed. This page has the same eleven sections in the same order as LDA, from the ground up. Sections 1, 2 and 8 are deliberately near-identical between the two — the shared ground is what makes the differences legible. If you have read the LDA page you can skim those three.

0The short version

Many different lines will separate two groups of points. SVM picks the one with the largest clearance on either side — the line that stays as far as possible from the nearest points of both classes. It makes no assumption about how the data were generated, no assumption of bell-shaped clouds, and no attempt to estimate how features covary. It looks only at the points nearest the boundary and ignores everything else. A single tuning parameter, C, controls how much it is willing to misclassify in exchange for a wider clearance.

1What one trial looks like to the model

Identical to the LDA page, because the input is identical — the models differ only in what they do with it.

A participant sees a word. We keep 10 electrodes and chop the second after word onset into 12 time bins, averaging the voltage inside each. That gives 10 × 12 = 120 numbers for that word. The model never sees a scalp or a waveform — it sees a list of 120 numbers, called x, with the count of entries called p = 120.

Each trial carries a label: later remembered or later forgotten. For SVM the labels are conventionally written y = +1 and y = −1 rather than 1 and 0. This is not a different problem, just a convention that makes the algebra tidier — multiplying by y then flips the sign for one class, which lets both classes be written in one equation.

A feature is one of those 120 numbers — one electrode at one time bin. The participant studies 225 words, so we have 225 pairs of (list of 120 numbers, label), of which about 202 are used for training on any given fold.

2What a weight is

Also identical to the LDA page. This is the shared ground.

The model produces one number per feature — the weights w1 … w120 — plus one constant b, the bias. To classify a new trial, multiply each feature by its weight, sum, add the bias:

score = w1x1 + w2x2 + … + w120x120 + b written compactly as score = wᵀx + b

Positive score, predict one class; negative, the other. A weight is a multiplier applied to one feature, and the set of them is the entire learned content of the model.

Geometrically: with two features each trial is a dot on a page and the rule "score = 0" is a straight line; with 120 it is a flat hyperplane in 120-dimensional space. The weights set the orientation, the bias slides it without rotating it.

So a trained LDA and a trained linear SVM are the same kind of object. Handed only the final numbers you could not tell which produced them. Everything that follows is about how the numbers were chosen.

One quantity SVM needs that LDA does not. The size of the weight vector, written ‖w‖ and read "the norm of w", is the square root of the sum of the squared weights. For LDA this is irrelevant — scaling every weight by 2 doubles all scores, flips nothing, and changes no prediction. For SVM the scale is load-bearing, because it is what sets the width of the clearance in §3.

3What SVM is trying to make true

Here the two models part completely. LDA assumed a generative story — Gaussian clouds of equal shape — and derived the optimal rule under it. SVM assumes nothing about how the data arose. It states a geometric preference and optimises for it.

The preference

If two groups of points can be separated by a straight line, they can usually be separated by infinitely many. Most sit awkwardly close to some point. SVM's claim is that the best choice is the one with the most room:

Choose the boundary that maximises the margin — the perpendicular distance from the boundary to the nearest training point of either class.

The reasoning is about generalisation rather than fit. A boundary squeezed against a training point will misclassify a future point that lands slightly on the wrong side. A boundary with clearance on both sides tolerates that jitter. Since every future trial is a noisy version of the same underlying thing, clearance is insurance.

The two traditions are less separate than they look. Cortes & Vapnik's paper — the one that introduced the method we use — opens not with margins but with Fisher (1936) and linear discriminant analysis. Their equations 1 and 2 are QDA and LDA; their first observation is that estimating a covariance takes on the order of p² numbers and that this is unreliable when observations are few. SVM was proposed as an answer to a problem the LDA page spends its §6 and §8 on.

Writing it down

Scores can be scaled arbitrarily, so we pin them: require that the nearest points score exactly ±1. Every training point must then satisfy

yi (wᵀxi + b) ≥ 1 for every trial i; the y = ±1 convention makes one line cover both classes

With that pinning, the total width of the clear strip works out to 2 ÷ ‖w‖. So making the margin as wide as possible means making ‖w‖ as small as possible. The whole training problem becomes:

minimise  ½‖w‖²  subject to yi(wᵀxi + b) ≥ 1 for all i Boser, Guyon & Vapnik (1992) — the hard-margin SVM

Read plainly: make the weights as small as you can while still getting every training trial right by a clear margin. Small weights mean a wide street.

This is a convex problem — bowl-shaped, one bottom, no local traps. There is exactly one answer and an algorithm will find it. That property is a large part of why SVMs became standard.

4How each weight actually gets its value

LDA computes its weights from two averages and a covariance — every trial contributes, in proportion to nothing in particular. SVM's answer has a completely different character, and it is the most surprising fact about the method.

The weights are a blend of a handful of trials

Solving the problem in §3 yields the weight vector in this form:

w = Σi αi yi xi a weighted sum of the training trials themselves

Each training trial xi gets a coefficient αi ≥ 0. The weight vector is those trials added up, each scaled by its coefficient and by the sign of its label. So an individual weight w47 is: take feature 47's value in each training trial, multiply by that trial's coefficient and label sign, and add them up.

And almost every coefficient is exactly zero

This is the part with the name attached. The solution has αi = 0 for every trial that sits comfortably on the correct side. Only trials on the margin boundary, or violating it, get a non-zero coefficient. Those are the support vectors.

Delete every trial that is not a support vector and retrain, and you get exactly the same classifier. The model is defined entirely by the points nearest the boundary. The comfortable majority contribute nothing.

The contrast with LDA is total. LDA's answer depends on the average of all trials, so a single outlier moves it a little and every trial moves it a little. SVM's answer depends only on the borderline cases, so most trials move it not at all — but a single badly-placed point right at the boundary can move it a lot.

Why the problem is usually solved in the other form

Rewriting the optimisation in terms of the α's instead of the w's — the dual form — has a useful consequence: the data only ever appear as dot products between pairs of trials, xiᵀxj. Never as individual features.

That opens the door to the kernel trick: replace the dot product with some other measure of similarity and you get a curved boundary, with no change to the algorithm and without ever constructing the higher dimensional space. A radial-basis kernel gives smooth blob-shaped regions; a polynomial kernel gives curved ones.

We use the linear kernel — the plain dot product — throughout, for a reason §8 makes clear: with 1.7 trials per weight, more flexibility is the opposite of what the problem needs. This lab has an RBF-kernel arm and it does not beat the linear one.

5A worked example you can check by hand

Two features, two trials, all arithmetic visible.

  • One remembered trial at x = (2, 2), label y = +1.
  • One forgotten trial at x = (0, 0), label y = −1.

By symmetry the best boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them, so w must point along (2,2) − (0,0), i.e. w = (a, a) for some a. Now apply the two margin conditions as equalities, since with only two points both are as close as possible:

(+1)(2a + 2a + b) = 1  →  4a + b = 1
(−1)(0 + 0 + b) = 1  →  b = −1

Substituting the second into the first: 4a − 1 = 1, so 4a = 2 and a = 0.5. The answer is

w = (0.5, 0.5),   b = −1 margin width = 2 ÷ ‖w‖ = 2 ÷ 0.707 = 2.83

Sanity check: the distance between the two points is √(2² + 2²) = 2.83. The margin is the full gap, which is right — with one point per class, the widest street is the whole space between them.

Reading off the coefficients

Both points are support vectors. Using w = Σ αi yi xi: only the first point is non-zero (the second is at the origin), so w = α1(+1)(2, 2) = (0.5, 0.5), giving α1 = 0.25. A further condition of the solution requires Σαi yi = 0, so α2 = 0.25 as well.

Now add a third trial that changes nothing

Put another remembered trial at (3, 3). Its score is 0.5×3 + 0.5×3 − 1 = 2, comfortably past the required 1. Its constraint is slack, so its coefficient is zero, and the boundary does not move. You could delete it and refit and get an identical model.

That is the whole personality of the method in one example. The trial at (3,3) is the most clearly remembered trial in the set, and it has no influence at all. LDA would have used it — it pulls the remembered mean outward. SVM discards it as uninformative about where the boundary belongs.

6The knob: C, and what happens when classes overlap

Section 3 assumed the two classes can be separated perfectly. Real data — certainly ours, where the effect is worth about 0.53 AUC — cannot be. The constraints of §3 have no solution at all, and the method as stated simply fails.

Allowing violations, at a price

Cortes & Vapnik (1995) fixed this, and it is the contribution that made SVMs usable. Give every trial a permitted amount of violation ξi ≥ 0 ("slack"), and charge for it:

minimise  ½‖w‖² + C Σi ξi   subject to yi(wᵀxi + b) ≥ 1 − ξi Cortes & Vapnik (1995) — the soft-margin SVM, and the citation to use

Two competing terms. The first wants a wide margin. The second wants few and small violations. C sets the exchange rate.

Cwhat the model prefersresult
smallviolations are cheap; keep the margin widea smooth, stable boundary that tolerates errors; many support vectors
largeviolations are expensive; classify the training dataa boundary contorted to catch every point; overfits
→ ∞no violations allowed at allback to §3, and no solution if the classes overlap

Small C is the regularised end, which is the direction you want with scarce data. This is the counterpart of LDA's Γ: both trade fidelity to the training data against stability, and both should be pushed toward stability when n is small. They are not, however, the same knob in disguise — Γ regularises an estimate of the noise structure, while C regularises the size of the weights. LDA has no notion of tolerated errors, and SVM has no notion of covariance.

The hinge loss, which is what that really says

The slack formulation is equivalent to charging each trial

loss = max(0, 1 − yi(wᵀxi + b)) the hinge loss

Zero cost once a trial is correct by a full margin; then rising linearly the wronger it gets. Two consequences worth holding onto: being very right earns no reward (the flat part), and being very wrong costs proportionally, not catastrophically — which makes SVM more robust to a wild outlier than a squared-error method, though less robust than one might hope, since a mislabelled point near the boundary becomes a support vector and gets full say.

7Reading the weights — the same trap, plus one more

Everything the LDA page says in its §7 applies here unchanged, and for the same reason. The weight vector is a filter — a recipe for extracting a signal — not a pattern showing where the signal is. A feature with no class information can carry a large weight because it provides a clean measurement of a contaminant worth subtracting.

Haufe et al. (2014) covers linear models generally, SVM included, so the same correction applies: to obtain an interpretable map, multiply the weights by the data covariance. Do not plot an SVM weight vector on a head and call it a brain map.

The extra trap: scaling

SVM is not scale-invariant, and LDA is. Because the objective penalises ‖w‖ — treating every weight as equally costly — a feature measured in larger units needs a smaller weight, and is therefore penalised less for the same real effect. Change a feature's units and you change the model. LDA has no such problem, since Σ−1 already normalises by each feature's variance.

In practice this means SVM features should be standardised before training, and our pipeline does it — fitcsvm is called with 'Standardize', true. It is not cosmetic. It also means an SVM weight is doubly uninterpretable: it reflects the feature's units as well as its role in the filter.

And the scores are not probabilities

LDA's score can be converted to a calibrated probability, because the model comes with a probabilistic story. SVM's cannot — the number is a signed distance from a boundary, and nothing in the method makes it a probability. Converting one requires fitting an extra calibration step afterwards (Platt scaling), which needs its own held-out data.

For us this is harmless: we score everything with AUC, which depends only on the ordering of the scores and not on their values. AUC is the right measure for exactly this reason.

8What breaks when trials are scarce

This section appears on both deep-dive pages, because the problem is shared — but the way each model suffers is different, and that difference is the useful part.

The classifier has to pin down 120 weights from about 202 trials. For LDA the damage is concentrated in one place: the 7,260-entry covariance matrix it cannot estimate. SVM never estimates a covariance, so it might seem immune. It is not — it fails a different way.

LDASVM
what it must estimate 2 means + a 120 × 120 covariance (7,260 numbers) nothing statistical — it solves a geometry problem
how few trials hurt the covariance estimate degrades systematically, and inverting it amplifies the error the nearest few points are a small, noisy sample, so the margin is placed by accident
the remedy shrinkage (Γ → 1) stronger regularisation (C → small)
which trials decide it all of them, through two averages only the borderline ones

SVM's specific vulnerability: with overlapping classes and few trials, nearly every trial becomes a support vector, so the boundary is set by a large number of individually unreliable points sitting in the overlap region. The property that makes SVM elegant when classes are well separated — depending only on the hard cases — becomes a liability when almost everything is a hard case.

Cortes & Vapnik make this precise, and the result is worth stating because it turns that paragraph from an intuition into a bound. For separable data they show that the expected error rate is bounded by the fraction of training trials that end up as support vectors:

E[Pr(error)] ≤ E[number of support vectors] ÷ number of training trials Cortes & Vapnik (1995), Eq. 5

Notice what the bound does not contain: the number of features. That is the striking claim of the method — a boundary held up by few points generalises well even in an enormous space, and they report ratios as low as 0.03 on real problems.

Now apply it to us. Our classes overlap almost completely — the effect is worth about 0.53 AUC — so nearly every trial sits in the contested region and becomes a support vector. The ratio approaches 1, and the bound degrades to "the error rate is at most 100%": true, and empty. The guarantee that justifies SVM is strongest exactly where we need it least, and silent exactly where we need it most.

Raudys & Jain (1991) analysed the classical family and found that trials needed grow linearly with features for any linear classifier — about 4 × features for full LDA, 1.2 × for a means-only rule. Their table does not cover SVM, which postdates it, but the linear-in-p scaling is a property of linear models in general and our own simulations reproduce it for both.

9Assumptions, variants, and failure modes

What SVM assumes

Strikingly little. No distributional assumption, no equal-covariance requirement, no requirement that features be Gaussian. This is its main advantage over LDA in principle — and it matters here, since our features have kurtosis around 23 against a Gaussian's 3, a substantial violation of what LDA wants.

But "assumes less" is not "needs less data". Assumptions are how a model gets by on fewer trials: LDA's Gaussian story is a strong constraint that does real work when data are scarce. Dropping it means nothing fills the gap. This is the standard trade, and at 1.7 trials per weight it does not obviously favour SVM — which is consistent with what we see, the two landing within about 0.01 AUC of each other.

Where it genuinely fails

  • Unstandardised features — see §7. A silent, serious failure.
  • Heavily unbalanced classes. The majority class dominates the total slack penalty and the boundary is pushed into the minority. Per-class C weights are the fix; our pipeline instead balances by resampling.
  • Mislabelled points near the boundary. They become support vectors and get full influence. A mislabelled point far from the boundary is harmless to LDA-with-averaging and expensive to SVM.
  • Kernel choice with scarce data. An RBF kernel can fit anything and, with 1.7 trials per weight, will.

Practical notes

  • It is not deterministic in practice. The solver fitcsvm uses is Platt's SMO (1998), which is sensitive to its random seed and to the library version. This is not academic for us: our committed SVM reference file had to be regenerated after a seed was added, while the LDA reference has never moved.
  • It is iterative, not one-shot. LDA computes its answer in closed form; SVM runs an optimiser to convergence.
  • It is inherently two-class. More classes require several binary models combined.
  • Cost scales with trials, not features. Between roughly n² and n³. With 202 trials this is instant either way.

The honest summary of LDA versus SVM here

On our data they perform almost identically, and the reason is the trial-count result: at 1.7 trials per weight both are dominated by estimation noise, and the difference between two reasonable estimators is small compared with the noise both are drowning in. Choosing between them is not where the gains are.
